\begin{abstract}
Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is widely used to reduce dimensionality by finding orthogonal directions of maximum variance. Independent Component Analysis (ICA) addresses a different question: can observed signals be expressed as mixtures of latent sources that are statistically independent? This report explains that distinction and demonstrates FastICA on three mono audio mixtures. The experiment uses 264,515 samples per observation at 44.1 kHz. FastICA converges in four iterations, produces linearly uncorrelated components, and reconstructs the observations with a relative root mean square error of approximately $4.22\times10^{-16}$. These values validate the numerical factorization, but they do not prove perfect source recovery because the original isolated sources are unavailable. The code, input mixtures, and separated audio outputs are provided in the companion GitHub repository.
\end{abstract}

\section{Motivation: from data compression to source separation}

Multivariate observations often combine information from several latent phenomena. In audio, multiple microphones can record different linear combinations of the same speakers or instruments. In electroencephalography, each electrode measures a mixture of neural activity and artifacts. Similar mixture models arise in telecommunications, biomedical sensing, and exploratory data analysis.

PCA and ICA are both linear representation methods, but they optimize different criteria. PCA is appropriate when the objective is compression, visualization, denoising, or variance preservation. ICA is appropriate when the objective is to recover latent signals that are as statistically independent as possible \cite{comon1994,hyvarinen2000}. The classic audio illustration is the \emph{cocktail party problem}: several sources are heard simultaneously by several microphones, and the goal is to estimate the individual sources from the recordings alone.

\section{PCA and ICA: related tools, different objectives}

\subsection{Principal Component Analysis}

For centered observations $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^m$, PCA finds orthonormal directions that diagonalize the covariance matrix. The first principal component maximizes projected variance, the second maximizes the remaining variance subject to orthogonality, and so on \cite{jolliffe2002}. If $\mathbf{U}$ contains the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix, the PCA scores are
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{z}=\mathbf{U}^{\mathsf T}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}).
\end{equation}
The PCA scores are linearly uncorrelated. This is a second-order property determined by covariances. Uncorrelated variables are not necessarily statistically independent, except under special distributional assumptions such as joint Gaussianity.

\subsection{Independent Component Analysis}

ICA starts from the linear instantaneous mixing model
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{x}(t)=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{s}(t),
    \label{eq:mixing}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{s}(t)$ contains unknown sources, $\mathbf{x}(t)$ contains observed mixtures, and $\mathbf{A}$ is an unknown mixing matrix. ICA estimates an unmixing matrix $\mathbf{W}$ such that
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{y}(t)=\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}(t)\approx\mathbf{s}(t).
    \label{eq:unmixing}
\end{equation}

The standard identifiable model requires mutually independent sources, a full-rank linear mixture, and at most one Gaussian source. In the determined case used here, the number of observations equals the number of sources. ICA also has unavoidable ambiguities: recovered components may appear in any order, with arbitrary sign and scale. These ambiguities do not indicate algorithmic failure.

\subsection{Why PCA alone does not solve source separation}

\begin{table}[htbp]
    \centering
    \small
    \caption{Conceptual comparison of PCA and ICA.}
    \label{tab:pca-ica}
    \begin{tabular}{>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.22\textwidth} >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.34\textwidth} >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.34\textwidth}}
        \toprule
        Criterion & PCA & ICA \\
        \midrule
        Main objective & Maximize explained variance & Minimize statistical dependence \\
        Output relation & Orthogonal and uncorrelated & As independent as possible \\
        Statistics used & Primarily second-order covariance & Higher-order or information-theoretic structure \\
        Typical use & Compression, denoising, visualization & Blind source separation, artifact removal \\
        Component order & Ranked by explained variance & No intrinsic ranking \\
        Ambiguities & Sign & Permutation, sign, and scale \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

PCA is nevertheless useful inside many ICA pipelines. Centering and PCA whitening first remove linear correlations and rescale the retained directions. ICA then rotates the whitened data to seek stronger statistical independence. In other words, PCA can be a preprocessing step for ICA, but decorrelation alone is not the final objective.

\section{FastICA}

FastICA uses fixed-point iterations to maximize a contrast related to non-Gaussianity \cite{hyvarinen1999}. The central-limit intuition is that a sum of independent variables is often more Gaussian than the original variables. Searching for non-Gaussian projections can therefore reveal the underlying sources.

For a standardized projection $y=\mathbf{w}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{x}$, non-Gaussianity may be approximated through negentropy. The implementation used in this project selects the smooth nonlinearity $G(y)=\log\cosh(y)$. After each update, the estimated directions are normalized and decorrelated. The parallel FastICA variant estimates all components simultaneously.

The experiment uses the \texttt{FastICA} implementation from scikit-learn with explicit unit-variance whitening \cite{sklearnfastica}. This explicit setting avoids version-dependent behavior and gives every estimated source unit variance before audio export.

\section{Reproducible audio demonstration}

\subsection{Data}

The repository contains three precomputed mono WAV mixtures named \texttt{ICA mix 1.wav}, \texttt{ICA mix 2.wav}, and \texttt{ICA mix 3.wav}. Each file has the following properties:
\begin{itemize}
    \item sampling rate: 44,100 Hz;
    \item resolution: 16-bit PCM;
    \item duration: 5.998 seconds;
    \item samples used: 264,515;
    \item number of observations: three;
    \item numerical rank of the observation matrix: three.
\end{itemize}

The integer samples are converted to floating point values, aligned to the shortest recording, and centered independently. The resulting matrix $\mathbf{X}$ has shape $264{,}515\times3$, with samples in rows and observed mixtures in columns.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Observed dependence and estimated components}

The observed mixtures are strongly correlated. Their sample correlation matrix is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{R}_{X}=\begin{bmatrix}
1.000 & 0.846 & 0.784\\
0.846 & 1.000 & 0.972\\
0.784 & 0.972 & 1.000
\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}

FastICA converged in four iterations. The estimated components have an identity sample correlation matrix to the displayed precision. Their Fisher kurtosis values are $[1.401,-1.499,2.775]$, compared with $[-1.273,1.290,0.931]$ for whitened PCA scores in this run. The change in higher-order statistics illustrates that ICA applies a rotation beyond PCA whitening.

The near-machine-precision reconstruction error confirms that the estimated mixing and unmixing transforms consistently factorize the observation matrix. It must not be interpreted as an audio source-recovery score: inverse transformation reconstructs the same mixtures on which the model was fitted.

The three exported WAV files can be inspected in \texttt{outputs/separated/} and played directly from the notebook. Listening provides a useful qualitative check, but a rigorous separation score such as SDR or SI-SDR would require access to the original isolated sources.

\section{Limitations and responsible interpretation}

This demonstration establishes a reproducible ICA workflow, but several limitations matter:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The isolated reference sources and the original mixing matrix are not available. Exact recovery quality cannot therefore be quantified.
    \item Zero correlation is weaker than statistical independence. PCA already demonstrates this by producing uncorrelated scores.
    \item Instantaneous linear ICA does not explicitly model reverberation, propagation delays, nonlinear mixing, or time-varying rooms.
    \item At least as many sufficiently distinct observations as sources are normally required in this determined formulation. Duplicating one recording does not create new information.
    \item ICA cannot uniquely identify multiple Gaussian sources without additional structure.
\end{itemize}

These qualifications replace the stronger claim in the draft report that the original instruments were objectively and perfectly recovered. The available evidence supports numerical convergence, decorrelation, non-Gaussian component estimation, reconstruction consistency, and qualitative listening - not a reference-based separation score.

\section{Conclusion}

PCA and ICA answer different questions. PCA finds an orthogonal representation that preserves variance and is ideal for dimensionality reduction. ICA seeks latent components with stronger statistical independence and is therefore suited to blind source separation. In the audio demonstration, PCA whitening removes second-order correlations, while FastICA performs an additional rotation guided by non-Gaussianity. The notebook converges rapidly, produces three separated audio files, and reconstructs the observations to numerical precision.
