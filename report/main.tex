\begin{abstract}
Signals encountered in audio, biomedical sensing, telecommunications, and finance are often mixtures of several latent phenomena. Independent Component Analysis (ICA) is a statistical framework for estimating latent components that are as independent as possible from multivariate observations. This report preserves the theoretical development of the original study and extends it with a direct comparison to Principal Component Analysis (PCA), mathematical derivations of whitening and FastICA, and a reproducible audio experiment. Three mono mixtures containing 264,515 samples each at 44.1 kHz are processed with FastICA. The algorithm converges in four iterations, yields linearly uncorrelated components with distinct higher-order statistics, and reconstructs the observations with a relative root mean square error of $4.219\times10^{-16}$. Because isolated reference sources are unavailable, these measurements validate numerical consistency rather than perfect source recovery. The complete notebook and audio files are available in the companion GitHub repository.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Signals are ubiquitous and frequently overlap. Voices recorded in a room, readings collected by multiple sensors, or electrical potentials measured on the scalp can each contain contributions from several underlying sources. Separating those sources is essential when the mixtures obscure the information of interest.

\emph{Blind source separation} seeks to recover latent signals while knowing little or nothing about the sources or the physical mixing process. Independent Component Analysis (ICA) provides a principled solution when the observations can be modeled as linear mixtures of statistically independent, mainly non-Gaussian sources \cite{comon1994,hyvarinen2000}. Instead of relying only on covariance, ICA exploits higher-order and information-theoretic structure.

This distinction is important because Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and ICA are sometimes confused. PCA identifies orthogonal directions that preserve variance and is extremely useful for compression, visualization, and denoising. ICA asks a different question: which latent directions make the recovered signals as independent as possible? PCA may prepare the data through whitening, but independence is stronger than decorrelation.

This report develops the ICA model and its assumptions, derives the main mathematical criteria used for estimation, compares ICA with PCA, reviews representative applications, and concludes with a fully reproducible audio demonstration using FastICA.

\section{Theoretical foundation of ICA}

\subsection{Linear instantaneous mixing model}

Let the unknown source vector be
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{s}(t)=[s_1(t),s_2(t),\ldots,s_n(t)]^{\mathsf T},
\end{equation}
and let the observed mixtures be
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{x}(t)=[x_1(t),x_2(t),\ldots,x_m(t)]^{\mathsf T}.
\end{equation}
The basic ICA model assumes an instantaneous linear mixture
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{x}(t)=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{s}(t),
    \label{eq:mixing}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ is an unknown mixing matrix. Each coefficient $a_{ij}$ represents the contribution of source $j$ to observation $i$.

In the determined setting, $m=n$ and $\mathbf{A}$ is invertible. ICA estimates an unmixing matrix $\mathbf{W}$ so that
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{y}(t)=\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}(t)
    =\mathbf{W}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{s}(t)\approx\mathbf{s}(t).
    \label{eq:unmixing}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Statistical independence}

The central assumption is that the sources are mutually independent. For all admissible values,
\begin{equation}
    p_{s_1,\ldots,s_n}(s_1,\ldots,s_n)
    =\prod_{i=1}^{n}p_{s_i}(s_i).
    \label{eq:independence}
\end{equation}
Independence implies zero covariance when moments exist, but the converse is generally false. Two variables can be uncorrelated while remaining dependent through nonlinear or higher-order relationships. This is precisely why diagonalizing a covariance matrix with PCA does not, in general, solve the ICA problem.

\subsection{Non-Gaussianity and identifiability}

The classical linear ICA model permits at most one Gaussian source. A multivariate Gaussian distribution that has been whitened is rotationally symmetric: any orthogonal rotation produces the same distribution. Therefore covariance and Gaussian likelihood alone cannot identify a unique rotation. Non-Gaussian source distributions break this rotational ambiguity and make the independent directions identifiable, apart from unavoidable permutation and scaling ambiguities \cite{hyvarinenbook}.

The central-limit intuition is that a normalized sum of independent random variables tends to be more Gaussian than the original variables. ICA algorithms can therefore search for projections that are maximally non-Gaussian.

\subsection{Intrinsic ambiguities}

Even under ideal assumptions, ICA cannot determine the original component order because
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{A}\mathbf{s}=(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{P}^{-1})(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{s})
\end{equation}
for any permutation matrix $\mathbf{P}$. It also cannot determine component scale because a source may be multiplied by a nonzero scalar if the corresponding column of $\mathbf{A}$ is divided by the same scalar. Consequently, sign changes, scale changes, and component permutations do not indicate failure.

\section{Mathematical criteria and estimation}

\subsection{Centering and PCA whitening}

ICA is usually applied after centering and whitening. If $\boldsymbol{\mu}=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}]$, centering gives
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{x}_c=\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}.
\end{equation}
Let the covariance eigendecomposition be
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{C}_x=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_c\mathbf{x}_c^{\mathsf T}]
    =\mathbf{E}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{E}^{\mathsf T}.
\end{equation}
A PCA whitening transform is
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{z}=\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}\mathbf{E}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{x}_c,
    \qquad
    \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}^{\mathsf T}]=\mathbf{I}.
    \label{eq:whitening}
\end{equation}
Whitening removes second-order correlations and constrains the remaining ICA mixing transform to a rotation. It simplifies the optimization but does not by itself establish independence.

\subsection{Kurtosis}

For a zero-mean variable $y$, the fourth-order cumulant is
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{kurt}(y)=\mathbb{E}[y^4]-3\bigl(\mathbb{E}[y^2]\bigr)^2.
    \label{eq:kurtosis}
\end{equation}
A Gaussian variable has zero excess kurtosis. Positive and negative values characterize super-Gaussian and sub-Gaussian behavior, respectively. Maximizing the absolute kurtosis of standardized projections is one possible ICA contrast, although kurtosis can be sensitive to outliers.

\subsection{Negentropy}

Differential entropy is
\begin{equation}
    H(y)=-\int p_y(u)\log p_y(u)\,du.
\end{equation}
Among variables with equal variance, the Gaussian distribution has maximum entropy. Negentropy therefore defines a nonnegative measure of non-Gaussianity:
\begin{equation}
    J(y)=H(y_{\mathrm{Gauss}})-H(y)\geq0.
    \label{eq:negentropy}
\end{equation}
Because exact entropy estimation is difficult, FastICA uses robust approximations based on smooth nonlinear functions such as $G(u)=\log\cosh(u)$ \cite{hyvarinen1999}.

\subsection{Mutual information}

Mutual information measures dependence between the components of $\mathbf{y}$:
\begin{equation}
    I(y_1,\ldots,y_n)
    =\sum_{i=1}^{n}H(y_i)-H(\mathbf{y})\geq0.
    \label{eq:mutual-info}
\end{equation}
It equals zero if and only if the components are mutually independent under standard regularity conditions. For whitened data, minimizing mutual information is closely connected to maximizing component non-Gaussianity \cite{comon1994}.

\subsection{Maximum likelihood}

If $\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}$ and the source densities $p_i$ are modeled, the change-of-variables formula gives
\begin{equation}
    p_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{W})
    =|\det\mathbf{W}|\prod_{i=1}^{n}p_i(\mathbf{w}_i^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{x}).
\end{equation}
For $T$ samples, the log-likelihood is
\begin{equation}
    \ell(\mathbf{W})
    =T\log|\det\mathbf{W}|
    +\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{n}
    \log p_i(\mathbf{w}_i^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{x}(t)).
    \label{eq:likelihood}
\end{equation}
Maximizing this expression encourages an invertible transform whose outputs follow factorized source models.

\subsection{FastICA fixed-point update}

For whitened samples $\mathbf{z}$ and a unit vector $\mathbf{w}$, one FastICA update has the form
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{w}^{+}
    =\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathbf{z}\,g(\mathbf{w}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{z})\right]
    -\mathbb{E}\!\left[g'(\mathbf{w}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{z})\right]\mathbf{w},
    \qquad
    \mathbf{w}\leftarrow\frac{\mathbf{w}^{+}}{\|\mathbf{w}^{+}\|_2}.
    \label{eq:fastica}
\end{equation}
The parallel algorithm updates all rows of $\mathbf{W}$ and then applies symmetric decorrelation,
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{W}\leftarrow
    (\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{\mathsf T})^{-1/2}\mathbf{W},
\end{equation}
until the directions stabilize. The fixed-point approach is fast and avoids manually selecting a learning rate \cite{hyvarinen1999}.

\section{PCA versus ICA}

PCA and ICA are related linear methods, but their objectives and interpretations differ. PCA finds directions of maximum variance and orders them by explained variance \cite{jolliffe2002}. ICA searches for a rotation that reduces statistical dependence after second-order structure has been removed.

\begin{table}[htbp]
    \centering
    \small
    \caption{Conceptual comparison of PCA and ICA.}
    \label{tab:pca-ica}
    \begin{tabular}{>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.22\textwidth} >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.34\textwidth} >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.34\textwidth}}
        \toprule
        Criterion & PCA & ICA \\
        \midrule
        Objective & Maximize explained variance & Minimize statistical dependence \\
        Output relation & Orthogonal and uncorrelated & As independent as possible \\
        Statistics & Second-order covariance & Higher-order and information-theoretic structure \\
        Typical use & Compression, visualization, denoising & Blind separation, latent source discovery \\
        Ordering & Ranked by explained variance & No intrinsic ranking \\
        Ambiguities & Sign & Permutation, sign, and scale \\
        Gaussian data & Fully meaningful & Multiple Gaussian sources are not identifiable \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

A useful workflow is therefore: PCA centers, optionally reduces dimension, and whitens; ICA then estimates the independent rotation. The demonstration later in this report makes the distinction visible: both PCA and ICA produce nearly diagonal correlation matrices, so correlation alone cannot demonstrate that ICA has recovered independent sources.

\section{Applications of ICA}

ICA is useful whenever observations contain additive contributions from latent sources:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Audio processing:} separation of simultaneously recorded voices or instruments, speech enhancement, and microphone-array analysis.
    \item \textbf{Neuroscience:} removal of eye-blink, muscle, and line-noise artifacts from EEG or MEG, and exploratory decomposition of brain activity \cite{makeig1996}.
    \item \textbf{Biomedical signals:} isolation of physiological components and artifacts in ECG, imaging, and sensor recordings.
    \item \textbf{Image analysis:} feature extraction and decomposition of image collections into latent spatial patterns.
    \item \textbf{Telecommunications:} blind channel identification and source separation in multi-antenna systems.
    \item \textbf{Finance:} exploratory extraction of statistically independent factors from correlated asset returns, with careful attention to nonstationarity and interpretability.
\end{itemize}

The assumptions must always be checked against the application. Reverberant audio, propagation delays, nonlinear sensors, and time-varying mixtures require extensions beyond instantaneous linear ICA.

\section{Reproducible audio demonstration}

\subsection{Input data and preprocessing}

The repository contains three precomputed mono WAV mixtures: \texttt{ICA mix 1.wav}, \texttt{ICA mix 2.wav}, and \texttt{ICA mix 3.wav}. Each is a 16-bit PCM signal sampled at 44,100 Hz. The shortest common segment contains 264,515 samples, corresponding to 5.998 seconds. The $264{,}515\times3$ observation matrix has numerical rank three.

The notebook converts integer samples to floating point values, aligns all observations to the same length, and centers each channel. Three genuinely distinct observations are essential: duplicating one recording would produce a rank-deficient matrix and would not create enough information to recover three components.

\section{Experimental results}

\subsection{Dependence in the observations}

The observed mixtures are strongly correlated:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{R}_{X}=\begin{bmatrix}
1.000 & 0.846 & 0.784\\
0.846 & 1.000 & 0.972\\
0.784 & 0.972 & 1.000
\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
The large off-diagonal values show that the observations share substantial linear structure.

\subsection{Estimated independent components}

FastICA converged in four iterations. The estimated components have an identity sample correlation matrix to the displayed precision. Their Fisher kurtosis values are
\begin{equation}
    [1.401,\,-1.499,\,2.775],
\end{equation}
compared with $[-1.273,\,1.290,\,0.931]$ for the whitened PCA scores. The change in higher-order statistics illustrates the additional rotation performed by ICA after PCA whitening.

    \caption{Three components estimated by FastICA. Their order, sign, and amplitude are not directly comparable with unknown source labels because of ICA's intrinsic ambiguities.}
    \label{fig:ica-components}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Why the correlation plot does not prove independence}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/pca_ica_correlation_comparison.png}
    \caption{Correlation matrices of the observations, whitened PCA scores, and ICA estimates. PCA and ICA both decorrelate, demonstrating that zero linear correlation alone is not evidence of independent source recovery.}
    \label{fig:correlation-comparison}
\end{figure}

The plot provides an important theoretical demonstration: PCA scores are already uncorrelated, yet PCA only fixes the covariance structure. ICA uses non-Gaussianity to select a further rotation. Evidence for ICA must therefore involve the model assumptions, higher-order statistics, reference-based metrics when available, or careful qualitative analysis.

\section{Limitations and responsible interpretation}

The experiment supports numerical convergence, decorrelation, non-Gaussian component estimation, reconstruction consistency, and qualitative listening. It does not support a claim of objectively perfect recovery because:
\begin{itemize}
    \item the isolated reference sources and original mixing matrix are unavailable;
    \item zero correlation is weaker than statistical independence;
    \item the instantaneous model does not explicitly represent echo, propagation delay, nonlinear mixing, or a time-varying room;
    \item the method requires sufficiently distinct observations and a full-rank mixture;
    \item multiple Gaussian sources are not identifiable without additional structure.
\end{itemize}

These qualifications correct the stronger wording in the first report version while preserving its central theoretical motivation.

\section{Conclusion}

ICA is a fundamental method for discovering statistically independent structure in multivariate data. Its usefulness extends from the cocktail party problem to neuroscience, biomedical sensing, imaging, telecommunications, and exploratory factor analysis. The method relies on a linear mixture model, independence, non-Gaussianity, and sufficient observations.

PCA and ICA should not be treated as interchangeable. PCA identifies variance-preserving orthogonal directions and supplies the whitening step used by many ICA algorithms. ICA then exploits higher-order information to seek a statistically meaningful rotation. The audio demonstration makes this relationship concrete: PCA and ICA both decorrelate the mixtures, but only ICA explicitly optimizes a non-Gaussian independence contrast.
